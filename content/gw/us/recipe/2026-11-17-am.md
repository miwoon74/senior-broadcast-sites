When family celebrated a bit too much the night before, Grandma always prepared this crisp, colorful persimmon and daikon salad. With sweet persimmon, crunchy radish, and a gentle vinegar dressing, this refreshing Japanese dish has welcomed autumn for centuries.

## Where it comes from
In Japan, this autumn side dish is known as *kaki namasu*. A well-known Japanese proverb says, "When persimmons turn red, doctors turn pale," reflecting how much people valued the fruit as a symbol of seasonal vitality. Grandmothers traditionally tossed fresh persimmon with salted daikon radish, serving it as a light, tangy side dish to help soothe a tired stomach after a festive gathering or evening of overindulgence.

## Ingredients (serves 4)
| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Persimmon | 200 g (about 1 fruit) |
| Daikon radish | 200 g (about 7 oz) |
| Salt | 2 g (about ⅓ tsp) |
| Vinegar | 30 ml (about 2 Tbsp) |
| Sugar | 9 g (about 1 Tbsp) |
| Yuzu peel (optional) | 1 g |

## How to make it
1. Thinly julienne both the daikon radish and persimmon into matchsticks.
2. Sprinkle the salt over the shredded daikon radish, toss gently, and let it rest for 10 minutes. Squeeze out the excess moisture thoroughly.
3. In a small bowl, whisk together the vinegar and sugar until the sugar dissolves.
4. Add the drained daikon radish, persimmon, and yuzu peel (if using). Toss together until crisp, glossy, and evenly coated.

## The good parts
- Provides dietary fiber from fresh radish and fruit.
- Made with ingredients that naturally supply vitamin C.
- Crisp, clean texture that refreshes the palate.

## What science says
Persimmons naturally contain vitamin C and plant compounds called tannins, but there are no clinical trials in humans showing that persimmons relieve hangovers or accelerate alcohol breakdown. Science hasn't tested it yet—that's not the same as wrong. And Grandma had good reasons: juicy radish and sweet persimmon deliver gentle hydration, light natural sugars, and a mild, soothing flavor when your digestive system needs something refreshing.

## How to enjoy it
Serve a small portion as an autumn side dish alongside your regular meal.

## Just be careful about this
- **Do not eat large amounts of persimmon at once—especially older adults, people who have had stomach surgery, or those managing diabetes**
  Persimmons contain a specific type of soluble tannin called shibuol. When shibuol interacts with stomach acid, it can polymerize and bind with food fibers, forming hard, stone-like masses known as bezoars (phytobezoars). Older adults produce less stomach acid and often have slower stomach motility, placing them at higher risk. In severe instances, these masses become very hard and may require surgical removal.
- **Chew thoroughly**
  Swallowing large, unchewed pieces of persimmon makes it much easier for tannins and fibers to clump in the digestive tract.
