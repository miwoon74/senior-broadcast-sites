When the air turns dry and your throat starts to feel scratchy, do you remember Grandma quietly making something in the kitchen? In this Saturday's world comparison, our theme is the throat. Let's compare three grandma recipes: snow fungus dessert soup from China, baesuk (steamed pear with honey) from Korea, and daikon honey syrup from Japan.

## At a glance

| Country | Recipe | When it was made | Evidence | Safety |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| China | Snow fungus soup (yin er tang, 银耳汤) | Dry autumn weather | ★ | 🟢 |
| Korea | Baesuk (steamed pear with honey) | Coughs, a dry and scratchy throat | ★ | 🟢 |
| Japan | Daikon honey syrup (daikon ame) | Coughs, a scratchy throat | ★★ | 🟡 |

## 1. China — Snow fungus soup (yin er tang)

### Where it comes from
In China, this sweet dessert soup has long been eaten in dry autumn weather, said to keep the throat and skin moist. Snow fungus (Tremella, sometimes called white wood ear) is sold dried; when soaked and simmered slowly, the soup turns silky and soft.

### Ingredients (serves 4)

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Dried snow fungus | 10 g (about ⅓ oz) |
| Water | 1000 ml (1 L, about 4¼ cups) |
| Rock sugar | 30 g (about 2 tbsp) |
| Dried jujubes / red dates (optional) | 4 |

### How to make it
1. Soak 10 g of snow fungus in water for 1 hour.
2. Trim off the hard base and tear it into small pieces.
3. Put it in a pot with 1000 ml of water and the 4 jujubes and simmer on low heat for 40 minutes (only about 10 minutes of hands-on time).
4. Add 30 g of rock sugar and stir until it melts.

- What's good: It contains dietary fiber.
- How to enjoy: One bowl on a dry autumn day.
- What science says: Research on snow fungus polysaccharides is mostly lab work, and studies in people are lacking. It hasn't been proven, but it has been loved for generations as a gentle sweet bowl for the dry season.
- Today's grade: Evidence ★ / Safety 🟢

## 2. Korea — Baesuk (steamed pear with honey)

### Where it comes from
A Korean folk remedy from palace and home kitchens: a pear is hollowed out, filled with honey and whole black peppercorns, and steamed. Grandmothers made it for coughs and a dry, scratchy throat.

### Ingredients (serves 1)

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Pear | 1 (500 g, about 1 lb) |
| Honey | 21 g (1 tbsp) |
| Whole black peppercorns | 5 (0.3 g) |
| Jujubes (optional) | 2 |

### How to make it
1. Cut the top off the pear like a lid and scoop out the core.
2. Add 1 tbsp (21 g) of honey, 5 peppercorns and 2 jujubes, then put the lid back on.
3. Steam for 30 to 40 minutes.
4. Spoon it up together with the juice that collects inside.

- What's good: Studies in children suggest honey may ease coughing.
- How to enjoy: About one pear a day when you have a cough.
- What science says: There are no studies on baesuk itself. It's best enjoyed as a warm, juicy dish where the honey and warm juice soothe the throat.
- Today's grade: Evidence ★ / Safety 🟢

## 3. Japan — Daikon honey syrup (daikon ame)

### Where it comes from
An old Japanese home remedy for coughs: cubes of daikon, the large Japanese white radish, are left to sit in honey, and the syrup that forms is sipped.

### Ingredients (serves 4)

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Daikon radish | 150 g (about 5 oz; a 5 cm / 2-inch piece, cut into 1 cm / ½-inch cubes) |
| Honey | 100 g (about 5 tbsp, enough to cover the daikon) |

### How to make it
1. Cut 150 g of daikon into 1 cm (½-inch) cubes.
2. Put the cubes in a clean jar and pour in 100 g of honey.
3. Leave for half a day to overnight, until the juice comes out.
4. Take the syrup a spoonful at a time, or stir it into hot water.

- What's good: Studies in children suggest honey may ease coughing. Daikon also contains dietary fiber.
- How to enjoy: A few spoonfuls a day when you have a cough.
- What science says: There's almost no research on daikon itself for coughs. Honey, however, has a Cochrane review on acute cough in children: compared with doing nothing, it may help ease coughing and sleep (low-to-moderate certainty of evidence).
- Today's grade: Evidence ★★ / Safety 🟡

*1 tbsp = 15 ml*

## Evidence compared
Of the three, the daikon honey syrup has the most research behind it (through studies on honey). Snow fungus soup and baesuk are wisdom passed down through generations. Enjoy any of them as food, and if you keep feeling unwell, see a doctor.

## Please note
- Daikon honey syrup: Keep the syrup in the fridge and finish it within a few days. The water from the radish thins the honey, so it spoils easily at room temperature.
- Snow fungus soup: Some versions add goji berries. If you take warfarin, be careful with those, because there have been cases where blood clotting values rose after taking goji berries together with warfarin.

## For everyone
- Never give honey to babies under 1 year old (baesuk, daikon syrup): honey can contain spores of the bacteria that cause botulism, and babies under 1 have little defense against them.
- If you have diabetes, go easy on the sweet ingredients (all three): foods and drinks high in sugar can raise blood sugar quickly.
