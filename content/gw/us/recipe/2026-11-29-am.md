## 1. Ginger Milk Pudding (Guangdong, China)

A dessert eaten in Guangdong to warm the body. Pour hot milk over fresh ginger juice and, almost like magic, it sets into a soft pudding.

| Ingredients (serves 2) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Fresh ginger | 30 g (about 1 oz) |
| Milk (regular, not low-fat) | 250 ml (about 1 cup) |
| Sugar | 15 g (about 1½ Tbsp) |

1. Grate the ginger, squeeze out the juice, and divide it between 2 bowls.
2. Warm the milk and sugar to about 70–80°C (158–176°F), when small bubbles form at the edge of the pan.
3. Pour it from a height over the ginger juice, all at once.
4. Leave it untouched for 5–10 minutes until set.

- The good part: There are human studies suggesting ginger may ease nausea. You also get some protein.
- What science says: An enzyme in ginger that breaks down protein is known to set the milk proteins. Its warming reputation hasn't been studied, but it has long been loved as a warm dessert on cold days.
- Just one caution: If you get heartburn or an upset stomach, use less ginger. Ginger can cause mild side effects in some people, such as heartburn, abdominal discomfort or diarrhea.

## 2. Hot Spiced Grape Juice (Modern Twist)

An alcohol-free version of glühwein, Europe's warm spiced winter wine.

| Ingredients (serves 1) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Grape juice (100%) | 200 ml (about ¾ cup + 1 Tbsp) |
| Cinnamon stick | 1 (or 0.5 g ground cinnamon, ¼ tsp) |
| Cloves (optional) | 2 |
| Orange peel (optional) | 1 piece |

1. Put the juice and spices in a saucepan.
2. Warm on low for 5 minutes without boiling.
3. Remove the spices and pour into a mug.

- The good part: A warm drink that helps you get fluids. It uses an ingredient with vitamin C.
- What science says: The combination itself hasn't been studied, and cinnamon hasn't been clearly shown to help with any disease. Enjoy it simply as a warm cup on a cold day.
- Cautions: If you have diabetes, dilute the juice about half with water — 200 ml of grape juice has a lot of sugar and can raise blood sugar quickly. And don't have large amounts of cinnamon for a long time, especially if you have liver disease. Common cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which in large amounts can strain the liver. Ceylon cinnamon has very little coumarin.

## 3. Snow Fungus Soup (China)

A Chinese dessert soup eaten in dry autumn weather to keep the throat and skin moist. Snow fungus (white tremella) is a mild, jelly-like dried mushroom.

| Ingredients (serves 4) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Dried snow fungus | 10 g (about ⅓ oz) |
| Water | 1000 ml (about 4¼ cups) |
| Rock sugar | 30 g (about 2 Tbsp) |
| Dried jujubes (red dates, optional) | 4 |

1. Soak the snow fungus in water for 1 hour.
2. Trim off the hard base and tear it into small pieces.
3. Simmer with the water and jujubes on low for 40 minutes (about 10 minutes hands-on).
4. Add the rock sugar.

- The good part: It contains dietary fiber.
- What science says: Research on snow fungus polysaccharides is mostly lab-based; human studies are still lacking. It has long been loved as a sweet bowl for the dry season.
- Just one caution: Some recipes add goji berries — if you take warfarin, be careful. There are reports of blood-clotting test values rising after people took goji berries with warfarin.

## 4. Rasam (South India)

A tangy, peppery soup that South Indian families pour over rice when someone catches a cold. Tamarind is a sour fruit used for its tang.

| Ingredients (serves 4) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Tamarind paste | 15 g (1 Tbsp) |
| Tomatoes | 200 g (2 tomatoes, about 7 oz) |
| Water | 800 ml (about 3⅓ cups) |
| Black pepper | 2 g (1 tsp) |
| Cumin | 2 g (1 tsp) |
| Garlic | 10 g (2 cloves) |
| Ground turmeric | 0.5 g (a pinch) |
| Salt | 5 g (1 tsp) |
| Cooking oil | 10 ml (2 tsp) |
| Mustard seeds (optional) | 2 g (½ tsp) |

1. Crush the pepper, cumin and garlic.
2. Add the tamarind, tomatoes, crushed spices, turmeric and salt to the water and simmer for 10 minutes.
3. In a small pan, fry the mustard seeds in the oil and pour over the soup.

- The good part: A warm broth that helps you get fluids. Tangy and a little spicy, it makes rice easy to eat when you have no appetite.
- What science says: Rasam itself hasn't been studied. We share it as a traditional dish whose hot, spicy broth warms you up.
- Just one caution: If you have gastritis or acid reflux, use less pepper. Hot spices like pepper can irritate the stomach and esophagus.

## 5. Apple Cinnamon Tea (Germany)

A fruit-and-spice tea German families love in winter.

| Ingredients (serves 1) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Apple | 50 g (¼ apple, thinly sliced) |
| Cinnamon stick | 1 (or 0.5 g ground cinnamon, ¼ tsp) |
| Hot water | 200 ml (about ¾ cup + 1 Tbsp) |
| Honey (optional) | 7 g (1 tsp) |

1. Thinly slice the apple.
2. Simmer the apple, cinnamon and water in a saucepan for 5 minutes (or pour hot water over them in a cup and steep for 10 minutes).
3. Strain and stir in the honey.

- The good part: Studies in children suggest honey may ease coughs. A warm drink that helps you get fluids.
- What science says: Cinnamon hasn't been clearly shown to help with diabetes, weight or any other condition. Food amounts are generally safe.
- Just one caution: Don't have large amounts of cinnamon for a long time, especially if you have liver disease. Cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which in large amounts can strain the liver. Ceylon cinnamon has very little.

## This Week's Takeaway

- Feeling chilled: ginger milk pudding, hot spiced grape juice
- Dry weather: snow fungus soup
- A cold coming on: rasam
- Crisp days, after meals: apple cinnamon tea

All five this week are ★ 🟢. They haven't been proven by research, but they're the wisdom grandmothers have made on days just like these, for generations. Why not pick one and make it tonight?

## Cautions for Everyone

- If you have diabetes, cut back on the sweet ingredients (sugar, rock sugar, grape juice). Sugary foods and drinks can raise blood sugar quickly.
- If you have high blood pressure, make the rasam less salty and have less broth. Too much salt can raise blood pressure.
- Never give honey to babies under 1 year old. Honey can contain botulism spores, and babies under 1 can't fight them off well.
