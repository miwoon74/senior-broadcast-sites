Many seniors choose between Medicare Advantage plans and traditional Medicare each year. A key fact to start with: in 2025, **about 30.5 million** people were enrolled in Medicare Advantage plans versus **about 22.1 million** in traditional Medicare. That difference matters for benefits, network access and potential costs.

## What differs between the two paths

- Extra benefits: Reporting notes that Medicare Advantage plans often add extra benefits such as vision, dental and fitness (examples cited in the New York Times analysis). These built‑in extras can be attractive to beneficiaries looking for broader, plan‑organized benefits.
- Networks and access: The same reporting highlights that Medicare Advantage plans commonly use narrower provider networks. That can mean a beneficiary’s usual doctor may be out of network unless the plan includes them.
- Prior authorization and utilization rules: The New York Times reports that some Medicare Advantage plans require prior authorization for certain services — a process that can affect timely access to specialists or procedures.

Because the broadcast source is a news analysis using CMS enrollment data, the descriptions above summarize what the reporting identifies as the main differences; exact rules, covered services and network breadth vary by plan.

## Why these differences matter to seniors

Plan structure affects practical aspects of care:

- Seeing your regular doctor: If your provider is out of a plan’s network, you may face higher costs or need to change clinicians.
- Surprise authorizations or denials: Prior authorization requirements can delay care or require appeals.
- Value of added benefits: Built‑in extras (vision, dental, fitness) may reduce the need for separate supplemental products, but they do not guarantee access to specific providers.

Choosing between plans is therefore not just about premiums; it’s about the combination of network access, required approvals, and the value you place on additional benefits.

## How to evaluate plans in your area

Use these steps when comparing plans:

- Check networks: Confirm whether your primary doctors and preferred specialists participate in the plan’s network.
- Review drug coverage: Compare formularies if you take prescription drugs, to see whether your medicines are covered and at what tier.
- Compare out‑of‑pocket rules: Look at copays, coinsurance and any limits on services (including prior authorization rules).
- Use official tools: Use Medicare.gov’s Plan Finder and call 1‑800‑MEDICARE for assistance, as the New York Times and CMS reporting recommend.

Checklist for plan comparison
- ☐ Confirm primary care physician is in‑network
- ☐ Check top specialists you may need for network participation
- ☐ Compare drug formularies for your prescriptions
- ☐ Review prior authorization rules and appeals process
- ☐ Compare extra benefits (vision, dental, fitness) and how they are delivered
- ☐ Check premium and estimated out‑of‑pocket costs for expected care

## A practical example to keep in mind

The enrollment numbers show many beneficiaries are already in Medicare Advantage (30.5 million in 2025). If you or a family member is considering switching, use the Plan Finder and direct calls to plans and providers to understand whether a plan’s extra benefits outweigh potential network or authorization limits for your needs.

## International note (context, not a complete comparison)

Reporting contrasted the U.S. picture with systems such as the U.K.’s National Health Service, where a universal benefit package reduces reliance on private plan networks. The broadcast source used this to illustrate how the U.S. mix of public and private options creates different tradeoffs around provider access and added benefits.
