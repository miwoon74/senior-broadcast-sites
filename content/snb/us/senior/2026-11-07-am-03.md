## What the coverage says, in plain terms

The Tribune piece (Nov. 7, 2026) summarizes three themes:
- Health checks (vision, hearing, cognition, medication) influence fitness to drive.
- Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) such as lane‑keep assist and automatic emergency braking can lower crash risk.
- Planning the transition — early family conversations, occupational therapy driving evaluations, and mapping alternative transportation — reduces abrupt loss of mobility.

Below we convert those themes into concrete steps and how to find assessments or programs referenced in the story.

## How to check driving fitness: official-style checklist

- ☐ Get a vision check at least annually (eye chart, glare/contrast tests where available).  
- ☐ Ask your clinician about a hearing screen if you notice difficulty hearing horns, sirens or vehicle noises.  
- ☐ Review all medications with your prescribing clinician or pharmacist for side effects that impair attention, reaction time or balance.  
- ☐ Consider a cognitive screening when memory or attention decline is suspected; discuss results with the clinician in the context of driving.  
- ☐ Take an objective driving screening: free online options such as AAA’s Roadwise Review are mentioned in the Tribune; for a formal on‑road and clinical evaluation, seek a referral from your state DMV or an occupational therapy driving specialist.  
- ☐ If possible, choose a vehicle with ADAS features (lane‑keeping assist, blind‑spot alert, automatic emergency braking) and learn how those systems work for that make/model.

These steps reflect the practical recommendations reported in the Nov. 7, 2026 feature.

## Where to get formal assessments and help (pathways)

1. Primary care clinician or geriatrician — start here for medication review, vision/hearing referrals and cognitive screening.
2. State DMV — many states list occupational therapy driving assessment programs, rehabilitation driving clinics, and guidance on license reporting/renewal rules.
3. Occupational therapy driving evaluators — conduct clinic-based and on-road assessments and provide individualized recommendations.
4. AAA Roadwise Review — an online screening tool the Tribune lists as a free initial self‑assessment (tool listing; no publication date given).
5. Community resources — local transit agencies, volunteer driver programs and ride services can be mapped in advance as alternatives.

When you seek help, ask whether an evaluation is a screening only (informal) or a comprehensive clinical/on-road assessment that results in documented recommendations.

## Vehicle technology: what the Tribune highlights

The feature points to ADAS features now commonly discussed as risk‑reducing aids:
- Lane‑keeping assist / lane‑departure warning
- Blind‑spot warning / rear cross‑traffic alert
- Automatic emergency braking (AEB)

These systems are tools, not guarantees. Families and drivers should learn each system’s limitations (e.g., weather, road markings, system disengagement) and avoid overreliance.

## Planning the transition early: practical steps for families

- Start conversations before safety becomes an emergency. Frame it as planning to preserve mobility and safety.  
- Map concrete alternatives: bus routes, paratransit, volunteer driver programs, paid ride services and local community shuttles.  
- Trial a schedule using alternatives for routine trips (shopping, pharmacy, clinic) to test access and affordability.  
- If the person wishes, consider restrictions before full cessation: driving only during daylight, avoiding highways, or driving only vehicles with ADAS. Document these agreements and revisit them periodically.

## A short table of options families can compare

| Option | Typical purpose |
|---|---|
| ADAS‑equipped personal car | Reduce some crash risks; keep independence with training |
| Formal OT driving assessment | Objective, individualized recommendation for continuing or stopping driving |
| Online screening (e.g., AAA Roadwise Review) | Initial self‑check of reaction and vision‑related tasks |
| Community transit / paratransit | Substitute mobility for routine appointments and shopping |
| Volunteer driver programs / ride services | Flexible alternative for social and medical trips |

## One-page checklist for an older driver visit

- Bring a current list of medications.  
- Note episodes of disorientation, near‑misses, fender benders or new dents.  
- Record driving habits: times of day, routes, recent difficulties parking or merging.  
- Ask the clinician for referrals to a driving assessment program or occupational therapist if concerns arise.  
- If considering limiting driving, create a one‑month mobility plan using alternatives.
