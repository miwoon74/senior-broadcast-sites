Scam alerts are multiplying as holiday shopping ramps up. Consumer advocates and government trackers say fraudsters are sending realistic fake delivery notices and account‑suspension messages that push people to click links or call bogus support. A key figure to know: **Amazon received 360,000 consumer inquiries about suspicious messages in 2026**, and the Federal Trade Commission reports imposter scams caused more than **$1 billion** in losses nationwide in 2026.

## Why this matters to older shoppers

- Realistic messaging: Scammers mimic major retailers and customer-service language, increasing the chance a recipient will panic and act.
- High volume: Amazon handling 360,000 inquiries shows how widespread suspicious messages were in 2026.
- Large financial harm: The FTC’s figure — over $1 billion in imposter‑scam losses in 2026 — shows these scams are among the costliest forms of consumer fraud.
- Sophisticated follow-ups: Phishing pages and fake support agents can trick victims into giving payment details or transferring funds, including retirement savings.

## How these scams typically work

- Fake order or delivery notice (urgent text or email) pushing you to “verify” by clicking a link.
- Account suspension or unauthorized‑purchase alert demanding immediate action or payment.
- Refund/recall messages that ask for bank details so a “refund” can be issued.
- Impersonation of chat or phone support that convinces victims to reveal passwords or move money.

## Practical steps older adults can take now

- Never click links or tap numbers in unsolicited texts or emails about orders. Instead:
  - Open your browser or the retailer’s official app and sign in from there to check orders.
  - Use bookmarks you saved yourself or type the retailer address manually.
- If you already clicked a link or shared information:
  - Immediately change your password on the retailer and any reused accounts.
  - Contact your bank or card issuer about possible fraud.
  - Report the incident to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
  - Call the AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline at 1-877-908-3360 for guidance.
- Enable two-factor authentication on accounts where available.
- Ask a trusted family member or friend to review unexpected messages with you before acting.
- Keep software and phone operating systems up to date to reduce exposure to malicious sites.

Checklist — things to do if you get a suspicious retail message
- ☐ Do not click any link or call any phone number included in the message.
- ☐ Open the retailer’s official site or app yourself and check order history.
- ☐ If you entered payment or login details, change passwords and contact your bank.
- ☐ Report the message to the retailer’s official fraud reporting page.
- ☐ Report the incident to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
- ☐ Call AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline 1-877-908-3360 if you want help.

## Other countries’ responses (brief)

The United Kingdom’s National Cyber Security Centre and partners operate a Suspicious Email Reporting Service that has removed more than 170,000 fraudulent shopping domains impersonating trusted retailers. National takedown programs are one tool for reducing dangerous phishing infrastructure, but they do not stop every scam message reaching inboxes.

## Summary: what seniors should remember

Scammers are using believable retail messages to provoke urgent reactions. The scale in 2026 — 360,000 Amazon inquiries and over $1 billion in imposter‑scam losses reported by the FTC — means vigilance matters. The safest habit is to verify independently: never use links or numbers in unsolicited messages, check your account through the official website or app, and report any suspected fraud.
