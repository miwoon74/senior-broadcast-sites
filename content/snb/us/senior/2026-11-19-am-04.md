Could someone you have only met through a screen be quietly planning to empty your retirement account? Federal regulators are warning older Americans that the answer is increasingly yes. According to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), **annual reported losses to romance scams have surged past $1.14 billion**, with victims aged 60 and older suffering the highest median financial losses of any consumer age group.

While online dating and casual web games offer welcome companionship, organized criminal networks use calculated emotional manipulation to exploit loneliness. Understanding how these schemes operate is the most effective way to protect your hard-earned financial independence.

## By the Numbers: The Heavy Cost of Online Deception

Recent data from the FTC Consumer Sentinel Network demonstrates that romance fraud is among the most damaging financial crimes facing older consumers. Although younger adults report encountering online imposters more frequently, older adults experience substantially larger individual financial losses.

| Romance Fraud Indicator | Official Federal Benchmark |
| :--- | :--- |
| Annual Reported U.S. Losses | Over $1.14 billion |
| Age Demographic with Highest Median Loss | Adults aged 60 and older |
| Primary Payment Methods Demanded | Wire transfers, cryptocurrency, retail gift cards |
| Common Platforms Used by Scammers | Social media networks, mobile games (e.g., Words with Friends) |

Federal authorities point out that the true economic toll is likely much higher than reported figures, as intense personal embarrassment prevents many victims from coming forward to law enforcement.

## The Scammer Playbook: Grooming, Crisis, and Demands

Romance fraudsters follow an established, highly calculated script designed to lower emotional defenses before attempting extortion.

### 1. Fabricating a Credible, Distant Persona
Perpetrators systematically set up fraudulent profiles on popular social media platforms, dating websites, and mobile games such as *Words with Friends*. They frequently pretend to be American citizens working abroad—most commonly military personnel deployed overseas or civil engineers managing foreign construction projects. This narrative provides a convenient excuse for why they can never meet in person or connect via live video.

### 2. Rapid Emotional Grooming
Once initial contact is made, the scammer moves aggressively to build intense intimacy. They lavish attention, share fabricated secrets, and profess deep affection or love within days or weeks. This stage is designed to foster absolute trust and create an emotional dependency that blinds victims to irregularities.

### 3. The Manufactured Emergency
After emotional trust is established, the criminal introduces an urgent, high-stakes personal crisis. Common fabricated scenarios include:
- Urgent medical surgery or emergency hospital bills in a foreign country
- Customs fees, port clearance charges, or taxes on valuable cargo
- Stranded travel arrangements, such as sudden visa fees or last-minute plane tickets to visit the victim

### 4. Untraceable Payment Requests
The scammer invariably demands payment through methods that are difficult or impossible to reverse. They insist on wire transfers, cryptocurrency deposits, or retail gift card PIN numbers, claiming traditional banking channels are temporarily blocked.

## Immediate Steps to Defend Yourself

Genuine affection will never come attached to an urgent request for your bank balance, gift cards, or crypto transfers. If an online relationship turns toward money, federal agencies recommend taking decisive defensive actions immediately:

- **Run an Image Search:** Conduct a reverse image search on the person's profile picture using major search engines to determine if the photo belongs to someone else or has been stolen from public web pages.
- **Enforce the Video Rule:** Refuse to form financial or personal commitments with anyone who refuses to participate in a two-way live video call.
- **Never Send Irreversible Funds:** Never wire money, purchase retail gift cards, transfer cryptocurrency, or share your banking details with someone you have only interacted with online.
- **Cut Off Contact Immediately:** If an online contact asks for money or investment participation, immediately cease all communication and block their account across all platforms.

## Action Checklist for Families and Older Adults

- ☐ Review social media privacy settings on platforms like Facebook to limit outreach from strangers.
- ☐ Verify that profile images of online acquaintances are authentic using a reverse image search tool.
- ☐ Establish a personal rule: zero funds sent to anyone never met face-to-face in person.
- ☐ Contact your bank's fraud department immediately if you suspect you wired funds under false pretenses.
- ☐ Save all messages, transaction details, and profile links if you need to file an official fraud report.

If you suspect that you or a family member is being financially exploited by an online scammer, reporting the incident promptly can help authorities disrupt these networks. You can report fraudulent profiles and losses directly to the FTC at **ReportFraud.ftc.gov** or by calling **1-877-382-4357**. If funds have already been transferred, notify your financial institution's fraud unit immediately and file a cybercrime report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at **Ic3.gov**. For free, confidential, peer-supported guidance, call the AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline at **877-908-3360**.
