A tragic incident near the North Hollywood Metro station today, where a vehicle left the roadway and struck street vendors and pedestrians on the sidewalk, has left **3 people dead** and several others injured. The crash has once again focused national attention on pedestrian vulnerabilities along busy urban corridors. For older Americans navigating neighborhood streets on foot, roadway design and driver collision-avoidance systems remain matters of critical daily safety.

Federal transportation statistics reveal that pedestrian fatalities nationwide remain near forty-year highs, with older pedestrians bearing a disproportionate burden of the risk. Understanding these statistics, the emerging vehicle technologies designed to prevent crashes, and steps pedestrians and drivers can take can help protect older community members.

## Looking at the Numbers: Older Adults Face Heightened Risk

According to traffic safety analysis from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), pedestrian deaths across the country continue at elevated levels, exceeding 7,300 deaths nationwide in 2024. 

While adults aged 65 and older make up approximately 17 percent of the total U.S. population, they represent roughly 20 percent of all pedestrian fatalities.

## Vehicle Technology: How Pedestrian AEB Works

To combat rising fatalities in crosswalks and on sidewalks, safety advocates and federal regulators have pushed for standards requiring advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS). 

A key technology is Pedestrian Automatic Emergency Braking (AEB). Unlike basic collision warnings, pedestrian AEB utilizes sensors and cameras capable of:
- **Detecting Foot Traffic:** Identifying pedestrians in complex urban surroundings, transit hubs, and low-light conditions.
- **Providing Forward Collision Warnings:** Alerting the driver visually or audibly to an imminent hazard.
- **Applying Automatic Braking:** Automatically engaging the brakes to avoid or lessen the impact when a driver fails to react in time.

Federal safety regulators have pushed for Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards requiring automatic emergency braking systems capable of successfully stopping for pedestrians at speeds up to 45 miles per hour. These standards aim to protect vulnerable road users when vehicles inadvertently drift toward sidewalk areas or transit waiting zones.

## International Safety Standards: Japan and Europe

Pedestrian safety standards have been expanding internationally to protect aging populations:

- **Japan:** The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism has instituted regulations making automatic emergency braking mandatory on new passenger cars as part of its initiative to protect pedestrians.
- **European Union:** Under General Safety Regulation (GSR) rules established by the European Commission Mobility and Transport Directorate, advanced driver assistance systems featuring vulnerable road user (VRU) detection are mandatory on newly registered passenger cars to reduce intersection crashes.

## Practical Steps for Drivers and Pedestrians

Whether driving or traveling on foot, older adults and their families can take specific precautions to reduce road safety hazards.

### Safety Checklist for Vehicles and Pedestrians

- ☐ **Check active safety systems:** Enter your Vehicle Identification Number (VIN) at nhtsa.gov/ratings to verify whether your car is equipped with active forward collision warning and Pedestrian AEB.
- ☐ **Monitor recalls:** Regularly review safety ratings and recall alerts for your vehicle model by visiting nhtsa.gov or calling the NHTSA Vehicle Safety Hotline at 1-888-327-4236.
- ☐ **Calibrate your driving position:** Enroll in a CarFit program or an AARP Driver Safety course to ensure mirrors and safety sensors are properly adjusted for your physical mobility.
- ☐ **Remain vigilant near transit stops:** Exercise extra caution when standing near curbs, bus shelters, and train stations where multi-lane vehicle traffic merges.
- ☐ **Use designated crossings:** Choose signalized intersections with clear sightlines and adequate crossing intervals whenever walking in busy commercial districts.
