Caring for an older family member at home can be emotionally rewarding — but it can also be expensive. A 2026 AARP estimate cited in a NewsNation report finds that family caregivers often shoulder about **$11,500 per year** in direct caregiving costs. That number is a helpful single snapshot of the financial pressure many households face; below we break down what that figure means, why it matters for retirement security, and practical steps older adults and families can take.

Key point: the figure $11,500 (2026) represents a typical annual out‑of‑pocket burden reported by AARP analyses and summarized by NewsNation on November 6, 2026. The broadcaster and AARP identify the most common categories that add up to this total: transportation, medical supplies and equipment, home modifications, and lost earnings from reduced work hours.

## Why this matters for seniors and families

- Reduced savings and retirement security: Ongoing annual costs can deplete emergency funds and planned retirement savings.
- Income and work effects: Caregiving responsibilities often lead caregivers to reduce hours or leave paid work, affecting earnings and retirement contributions.
- Household budgets: Even a single‑year shortfall of the magnitude cited can force choices between essentials such as housing, medication, or food.

Because the AARP estimate is an average snapshot, individual household burdens vary widely depending on care intensity, local service availability, and whether public benefits apply.

## What goes into the out‑of‑pocket total

The broadcast and the AARP reference list these common expense categories (qualitative categories cited in the report):

- Transportation to appointments and errands
- Medical supplies and home health supplies
- Home modifications or durable medical equipment
- Lost wages from reduced work or leaving employment

The NewsNation summary and AARP materials emphasize that both direct payments and indirect losses (reduced earnings) contribute to the overall financial impact.

## What policy and program options exist (U.S. focus)

The report points to several public and community resources caregivers can explore:

- Medicaid home‑and‑community‑based services (HCBS): State Medicaid programs may cover in‑home care services for eligible recipients; rules and availability differ by state. Check your state Medicaid website for current program details.
- Employer supports: Family leave policies, flexible scheduling, and dependent care benefits can reduce income loss. Ask HR about available programs.
- Community programs: Respite services, adult day programs, and local nonprofit supports can reduce unpaid caregiving hours and related expenses.
- AARP resources: AARP publishes guidance and state‑by‑state information for caregivers (AARP caregiving resources page cited in the report).

The broadcast also noted Japan’s municipal caregiver subsidies and respite services as an international example of local measures to reduce household burdens; the NewsNation piece referenced Japan’s model without attaching specific dollar figures.

## Practical checklist for caregivers and families

- ☐ Track all caregiving‑related spending for one month: transport, supplies, equipment, and outlays for care.
- ☐ Estimate lost earnings from reduced hours or job changes and add to direct costs.
- ☐ Review state Medicaid HCBS eligibility and waiting lists at your state Medicaid website.
- ☐ Consult your employer about family leave, flexible schedules, or dependent care benefits.
- ☐ Contact AARP caregiving resources for guides and local supports: https://www.aarp.org/caregiving
- ☐ Speak with a financial planner or legal advisor about protecting retirement assets and exploring tax credits or benefit programs.

## How to use this number personally

Treat the $11,500 figure as a planning prompt rather than a prediction. If you or a family member expect to provide sustained care, run your own budget: compile likely monthly costs, account for possible work income reductions, and build a contingency fund. Use the checklist above and start conversations with employers, insurers, and local benefit offices early.
