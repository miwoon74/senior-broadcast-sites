Have you ever had your computer screen suddenly freeze with a blaring siren and an urgent warning that your system is infected? In 2025 alone, **older adults reported losing over $1.0 billion to tech support scams**, representing nearly half of the total losses recorded across the country. These frightening browser messages are engineered to create panic, but understanding how they operate can keep your money and personal data completely safe.

## The Numbers Behind Counterfeit Tech Support

Federal authorities, including the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and the FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), track the severe financial toll of these operations. In 2025, overall consumer losses tied to tech support scams surpassed $2.1 billion, while total imposter scam losses reached $3.5 billion across all categories. 

Already in 2026, federal reporting trackers have logged 61,000 formal fraud complaints related to these impersonation schemes. The data shows that older Americans continue to bear an outsized share of the financial devastation.

## Anatomy of a Tech Scam: Fake Locks and Remote Access

The mechanics of a tech support scam follow a predictable pattern designed to manipulate trust. Fraudsters often use deceptive web scripts or manipulate search results to display full-screen pop-ups that look like authentic alerts from Microsoft Defender, Apple, or Geek Squad.

1. **The Simulated Screen Lock:** A window fills your entire display, often accompanied by looping siren noises, robotic voice warnings, or countdown timers. It falsely claims your computer has been compromised by malware or that illegal activity was detected.
2. **The Urgent Toll-Free Number:** The pop-up insists you must call a specific phone number immediately to avoid permanent data loss or legal consequences.
3. **The Demand for Remote Access:** When you call, an operator posing as a certified technician claims they must inspect your files. They instruct you to download remote desktop programs such as AnyDesk or TeamViewer.
4. **The Theft of Funds:** Once inside your machine, criminals can view personal records, siphon stored passwords, access online banking portals, or demand payments through bank wire transfers, gift cards, or cryptocurrency kiosks.

Legitimate technology companies such as Microsoft and Apple never display telephone numbers on security pop-ups, never initiate unsolicited phone calls about device health, and never demand payment through gift cards or cryptocurrency. When a browser locks, your computer itself is almost never damaged; the scammer has merely trapped your internet browser window in full-screen mode.

## Immediate Steps to Take If Your Screen Freezes

If an alarming alert takes over your monitor, you do not need to call any number or pay a technician to inspect your hardware. Taking a deliberate pause is your strongest protection against cybercriminals.

- ☐ **Do not call the number on the screen:** Never dial a telephone number displayed inside an unsolicited web browser warning.
- ☐ **Force close your browser:** On Windows, press **Ctrl + Alt + Delete** simultaneously, select Task Manager, and end the browser program. On Mac, press **Command + Option + Escape** to force quit the application.
- ☐ **Perform a hard restart if needed:** If the keyboard shortcuts do not respond, press and hold your computer's physical power button for 10 seconds until the device powers off completely, then restart it.
- ☐ **Never permit remote access:** Decline any request from an unverified caller asking to take control of your mouse, screen, or files.
- ☐ **Report the fraudulent incident:** Document the interaction and report the deceptive phone number or website to federal regulators.

## Where to Report Fraud and Seek Assistance

If you suspect you have encountered an imposter scam or accidentally allowed someone access to your machine, prompt reporting helps authorities track criminal networks and mitigate losses.

- **Federal Trade Commission:** Submit an official fraud report at <https://reportfraud.ftc.gov>.
- **Phishing and Suspicious Emails:** Forward counterfeit renewal invoices or suspicious email messages to the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency at `phishing-report@cisa.gov`.
- **Department of Justice Elder Justice Hotline:** If you shared banking details or granted remote control of your computer, call 1-833-372-8311 for guidance and assistance.
- **AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline:** For free, confidential support and advice from trained fraud specialists, call 1-877-908-3360.
