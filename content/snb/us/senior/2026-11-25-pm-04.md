Looking for a peaceful, refreshing way to stay active, sharpen your mind, and connect with neighbors this winter? The annual Audubon Christmas Bird Count is officially opening registration across the country, inviting nature enthusiasts, retirees, and beginners alike to participate in a storied tradition. Entering its **127th consecutive year in 2026**, the nationwide wildlife census offers older adults a low-impact, rewarding pathway to enjoy nature right from neighborhood parks, paved community trails, or cozy living room armchairs.

Whether you are an experienced naturalist with professional binoculars or someone who simply enjoys watching birds visit your patio feeder, winter birding provides an accessible winter activity designed for all mobility levels.

## By the Numbers: A Century-Long Community Wildlife Census

The National Audubon Society’s Christmas Bird Count stands as the longest-running community science wildlife survey in the world. Organized entirely by volunteer compilers and grassroots nature lovers, the count harnesses observations from across the Americas to compile an essential scientific record. Ornithologists and conservation scientists rely on this vast historical baseline to monitor changing bird population trends, analyze winter migration patterns, and track shifts in local habitats.

During the 2025 census, more than 85,000 volunteers contributed their observations across roughly 2,700 designated counting circles throughout the Americas. Each local circle covers an established 15-mile diameter area where teams spend time identifying and tallying local bird species. 



As the data illustrates, tens of thousands of everyday observers power this expansive wildlife effort every winter. The structured format allows local Audubon chapters to pair beginner birders directly with seasoned observers, ensuring everyone feels welcome and supported regardless of their previous experience.

## Physical and Cognitive Benefits of Winter Birding

Staying physically active and socially engaged during the colder months can sometimes feel challenging. Winter birding serves as a gentle, restorative activity that addresses multiple aspects of healthy aging:

### 1. Gentle, Low-Impact Physical Exercise
Walking through local nature preserves, county parks, or community gardens delivers cardiovascular support without placing heavy physical stress on your joints. Birding naturally encourages gentle walking, gentle stretching, and sustained balance practice over varied terrain, protecting knees and hips from undue strain while keeping you moving outdoors.

### 2. Cognitive Engagement and Mental Acuity
Identifying distinctive bird plumage, listening carefully to winter song calls, and recording species tallies in logbooks offers a stimulating cognitive workout. Engaging your sensory focus and working memory to spot subtle field markings exercises visual attention and problem-solving skills. Studies emphasize that viewing natural greenspaces and engaging in visual wildlife identification reduces cortisol levels, relieves everyday tension, and promotes a calm, centered mindset.

### 3. Meaningful Social Bonds
Joining a local count circle builds bonds with fellow community members who share an appreciation for wildlife. Sharing discoveries, comparing notes on winter sightings, and working as a unified team counteracts winter isolation and strengthens local social networks.

## Accessible Counting: From Paved Trails to Living Room Windows

A common misconception is that bird counts require hiking long miles through rugged terrain or deep winter snow. In reality, the Audubon count is intentionally designed to welcome participants across all levels of physical comfort and mobility.

Local count compilers welcome dedicated backyard feeder-watchers who record bird visits directly from indoor spaces. You can participate fully while sitting by your kitchen window, relaxing in a comfortable living room armchair, or watching a feeding station within an assisted living community courtyard. Indoor feeder counts provide crucial scientific observations, documenting urban and suburban wildlife visits that field teams might otherwise miss.

For those who want to venture outside, many county park departments and National Wildlife Refuges coordinate guided, senior-friendly bird walks along level, paved, and wheelchair-accessible trails. These organized outings proceed at a relaxed, unhurried pace, ensuring every participant can observe wildlife comfortably and safely.

## Practical Steps to Get Started This Season

Getting involved is completely free and requires very little specialized equipment. Here is how you can prepare before count circles fill up:

1. **Locate Your Local Count Circle**: Visit the official National Audubon Society locator at audubon.org to find your nearest 15-mile circle compiler. Reach out early to sign up for a team that matches your preferred mobility level, whether you plan to walk an accessible trail or monitor your home feeder.
2. **Download Helpful Identification Tools**: Download the free Merlin Bird ID app developed by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology. The app uses your smartphone microphone or camera to assist you in identifying winter species by their calls or visual photos.
3. **Set Up and Clean Your Feeders**: If you plan to participate as a feeder-watcher, clean and restock your backyard bird feeders ahead of the count window to attract regional winter visitors.
4. **Connect With Community Recreation**: Reach out to your local county parks department, senior outdoor clubs, or adaptive recreation programs through the National Recreation and Park Association (nrpa.org) to discover local bird walks. For general community resources, you can also contact your local Area Agency on Aging at 1-800-677-1116.
