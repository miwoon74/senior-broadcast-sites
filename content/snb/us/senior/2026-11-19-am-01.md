Are you and your household prepared if heavy rain suddenly closes your street or cuts off neighborhood power? As the first major atmospheric river of the season makes landfall across Southern California, county emergency officials have placed foothill, mountain, and valley neighborhoods on alert. For older adults, especially those living downstream from recent wildfire zones, peak rainfall rates of up to **1 inch per hour** create severe hazards that demand advance preparation rather than last-minute reaction.

Steep driveways can quickly erode, low-lying street corners can flood within minutes, and high winds can disrupt electrical service needed for essential medical devices. Understanding the rainfall projections, knowing your regional risk zones, and taking early action will keep you and your family safe as the storm moves through the region.

## By the Numbers: Projected Rainfall Across Southern California

The National Weather Service has issued flood watches that span Los Angeles, Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo, and Ventura counties, remaining in effect through November 20, 2026. Official forecasts show significant differences in precipitation amounts depending on elevation and topography. 

While valley and coastal communities can expect substantial runoff, mountain ridges and foothill passes face the greatest precipitation loads. The storm is projected to deliver between 1 and 3 inches of water across lower elevations, while foothill communities and coastal mountain ranges face totals reaching 3 to 5 inches.

## Why Burn Scars and Atmospheric Rivers Threaten Foothill Homes

When intense wildfires burn through brush and trees, they leave behind scarred slopes where organic topsoil has been stripped away. In Southern California, recent fire perimeters—specifically the Palisades, Eaton, and Hurst burn scars—remain highly vulnerable to flash floods and debris flows. Without root systems to anchor the mountainside, soil, rock, and loose debris liquefy under sustained downpours.

Emergency managers emphasize that mudslides and debris flows move rapidly and without warning. When rainfall exceeds 0.75 to 1.0 inch per hour over burned hillsides, hillside soil quickly transforms into a thick slurry capable of moving boulders, sweeping away fences, and blocking access roads.

For older homeowners, these geomorphic hazards present distinct challenges:

- **Access Route Blockages:** Canyon roads and single-lane hillside streets can wash out or become impassable, trapping residents who depend on daily caregiver visits or meal deliveries.
- **Physical Demands of Property Maintenance:** Trying to clear blocked culverts or place heavy protective barriers once heavy rain begins exposes older adults to falls, hypothermia, and injury.
- **Electrical Outages and Assistive Technology:** Severe weather frequently downs tree limbs onto local power lines. Localized electrical outages can immediately disrupt electric lift chairs, home oxygen concentrators, and refrigerated medical supplies.

Because of these compounding factors, the Los Angeles County Office of Emergency Management issued targeted evacuation warnings beginning at 6:00 PM for designated hillside communities. Responders caution that individuals who require extra time to travel, use mobility aids, or rely on specialized transportation should never wait for mandatory orders before relocating to safer ground.

## Protecting Your Living Space and Maintaining Utilities

Preparing your home ahead of severe storm systems helps prevent expensive structural damage and preserves life-safety systems. Ground-level drainage paths must be inspected immediately before precipitation peaks.

Walk around your home's exterior to confirm that ground-level patio drains, channel grates, and exterior downspouts are free from accumulated leaves, dirt, and garden debris. When water cannot flow freely into municipal storm drains, it backs up toward foundations, patio sliders, and garages.

Outdoor furnishings, potted plants, and lightweight patio items should be relocated indoors or securely anchored. High wind gusts associated with atmospheric river fronts can turn unsecured patio items into hazards or push them into drainage ditches, causing rapid water accumulation around external walls.

Inside the home, focus attention on power dependency:

1. **Verify Medical Backups:** Confirm that auxiliary battery packs for home medical equipment are fully charged and plugged into surge-protected outlets.
2. **Lighting Alternatives:** Inspect flashlights and battery-powered lanterns, ensuring they are placed at bedside tables and near interior walkways to eliminate fall hazards during a sudden blackout.
3. **Preserve Device Batteries:** Charge mobile phones, power banks, and tablets fully before rain intensifies, keeping communication channels open if local utility lines fail.

## Safe Evacuation and Community Support Services

If local law enforcement or county deputies issue an evacuation warning for your neighborhood, do not wait until water pools around your vehicle tires. Evacuating during daylight hours along familiar roads is considerably safer than attempting to navigate winding, water-covered streets in the dark.

Assemble an emergency go-bag designed for quick departures. Your kit should feature a seven-day medication supply, emergency contact cards, spare glasses, essential mobility equipment chargers, copies of your Medicare card, and supplies for household pets. Keep this bag adjacent to your primary entrance alongside sturdy footwear and a warm, water-resistant jacket.

Older adults who need transportation assistance, local emergency shelter information, or community resources have access to several established regional networks:

- **Emergency Alert Registrations:** Register your landline, mobile telephone, and email address for official geolocated advisories through Ready.lacounty.gov.
- **Immediate Shelter and Transit Information:** Dial 211 to connect with local operators who can direct you to accessible storm shelters, non-emergency dispatch, and neighborhood relief hubs.
- **Care Coordination and Area Agencies on Aging:** Older residents seeking disaster planning guidance or local transit solutions can contact their local Area Agency on Aging by visiting eldercare.acl.gov or calling the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116.
- **Disaster Preparedness Standards:** Access comprehensive evacuation planning and shelter support guidance through the American Red Cross at Redcross.org or by calling 1-800-733-2767.

Never attempt to walk, roll, or drive through standing water. Roads that appear shallow can conceal collapsed pavement or fast-moving currents capable of carrying away passenger vehicles.

## Severe Weather Action Checklist

- ☐ Register landline and mobile phone numbers with Ready.lacounty.gov for localized notifications.
- ☐ Clear debris, leaves, and dirt from ground-level patio drains and gutter downspouts.
- ☐ Inspect auxiliary battery backups for power-dependent medical machinery and assistive devices.
- ☐ Pack a 72-hour go-bag that includes a seven-day medication supply and a copy of your Medicare card.
- ☐ Secure outdoor patio furniture and potted plants away from exterior drainage channels.
- ☐ Confirm an evacuation destination and travel route with family members or emergency contacts.
- ☐ Store flashlights and fresh batteries in accessible bedside locations.
- ☐ Keep pet carriers, pet food, and leash supplies near your vehicle or main entrance.

> Storm forecasts and evacuation advisories reflect data issued by the National Weather Service and the Los Angeles County Office of Emergency Management for the storm system active through November 20, 2026. For real-time evacuation zones, shelter sites, and road closures, consult your municipal emergency coordination office or dial 211.
