The Administration for Community Living (ACL) announced on Nov. 12, 2026 that it is awarding **$8.4 million** in grants to support research and services focused on life after spinal cord injury. The funding targets projects to improve independence, community integration, assistive technologies, rehabilitation access and supports for people aging with spinal cord injuries, and to help translate research into practical local services.

## Why this matters for seniors and caregivers

As people age, mobility needs and long-term care planning change. ACL says the grants prioritize work that addresses aging with spinal cord injury and the transition from clinical care to community-based supports. For seniors or family caregivers facing new mobility loss—whether from spinal injury or progressive conditions—the funded research and demonstration projects are intended to:

- Improve access to rehabilitation and ongoing therapies in the community;
- Support development and spread of assistive technologies suitable for home use;
- Inform models for home modification, training for caregivers, and transportation solutions when driving is not possible;
- Translate findings into policies and service models that state and local aging services can adopt.

ACL emphasizes collaboration with State Independent Living Centers, Area Agencies on Aging, and community partners to move research into practical local programs.

## How ACL intends to use the funding

According to the ACL announcement, the $8.4 million in grants will support projects across several mutually reinforcing areas:

- Development and evaluation of assistive technologies and tools that help people perform daily tasks at home.
- Expanded access to rehabilitation services and strategies for long-term functional maintenance after discharge from acute care.
- Community integration initiatives, including training and supports for caregivers, peer mentoring, and connections to local transportation and housing modification resources.
- Research and demonstration projects that inform policy and scalable service models for aging individuals with spinal cord injury.

The announcement stresses translation from research to local service systems by partnering with existing aging and independent living networks.

## What practical benefits might appear locally

Over time, ACL-funded projects can produce tangible improvements that matter to seniors and caregivers, such as:

- Better pathways for community-based rehabilitation referrals through Area Agencies on Aging or State Independent Living Centers.
- New or improved assistive devices that make self-care, transfers, and mobility at home easier and safer.
- Training materials for family caregivers on safe transfers and use of assistive equipment.
- Pilot models for coordinating paratransit, volunteer rides, and accessible-ride services tied to care appointments and errands.
- Evidence local service planners can use to apply for additional program funding or to change service delivery.

ACL’s stated goal is to ensure research leads to practical, adoptable solutions for local agencies and families.

## What you can do now — a short checklist

- ☐ Look up your State Independent Living Center and Area Agency on Aging via the Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov to ask about local rehabilitation referrals and home modification resources.  
- ☐ Ask your local transit agency about paratransit eligibility and accessible-ride options; many agencies provide enrollment forms and guidance online.  
- ☐ If you care for someone with a spinal cord injury or mobility loss, request information from local aging and independent living services about caregiver training and assistive-technology loan/demo programs.  
- ☐ Monitor ACL announcements (acl.gov) for summaries of funded projects and findings that may inform local services or potential pilot programs.  
- ☐ Share needs and service gaps with your Area Agency on Aging or State Independent Living Center—local input can influence which interventions are prioritized.

## How ACL’s work connects to local programs

ACL highlights partnership with existing networks—State Independent Living Centers and Area Agencies on Aging—for translating research into community practice. That means local aging services are likely to be the first places where new tools, training, or service models piloted with ACL support become available. If you want new services in your area, connecting with these agencies early is the most direct route to both learn about pilots and to advocate for adoption.
