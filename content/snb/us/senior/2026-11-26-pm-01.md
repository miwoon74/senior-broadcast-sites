## What the situation is, in plain terms
- The Associated Press reports heavy rain, wind and flooding across large parts of the country on Nov. 26, 2026, prompting road closures, postponed public events and some parade segments moved to broadcast-only formats. (AP, Nov. 26, 2026)
- National Weather Service briefings and flood warnings placed many corridors under flash-flood alerts, with local rainfall totals reported in the range of roughly 1 to more than 6 inches. (NWS, Nov. 26, 2026)
- The CDC highlights that extreme weather commonly causes power outages and infrastructure strain that interrupt refrigeration for medicines (for example, insulin) and operation of home medical equipment, raising risks for people with chronic conditions. The CDC guidance notes that outages on the order of 24 to 72 hours can be dangerous for people dependent on refrigerated medicines or electrically powered medical devices. (CDC, 2026)

## Why it especially matters for seniors
- Refrigerated medicines: Insulin and some biologic medications need refrigeration; loss of power for a day to several days can affect their effectiveness and availability.
- Home medical devices: Oxygen concentrators, suction machines, ventilators and power-dependent mobility aids may stop working during outages.
- Access to care: Flooded or closed roads can delay routine and emergency medical visits, and clinics or pharmacies may be closed or operating on limited schedules.
- Mobility and evacuation: Flooding and closed routes increase transport difficulty, particularly for those who do not drive or who use mobility devices.

## Practical steps for seniors and families — do these now
- ☐ Make a concise medicines-and-equipment list: include drug names, doses, refrigeration needs, and device model numbers; keep a printed copy in your emergency kit and a digital copy for a caregiver.  
- ☐ Check refrigerator plans for medicines: know where you could keep critical meds cool for 24–72 hours (coolers with ice, neighbor’s power, or a designated shelter). Follow CDC guidance for medicine and vaccine storage during outages.  
- ☐ Register with your utility’s medical-needs or priority service program if you rely on electricity for life-sustaining equipment; call or visit your utility’s official website to confirm enrollment details.  
- ☐ Have at least one backup plan for power: household backup, neighbor willing to host, generator (and safe-operation plan), or local shelter options. Confirm in advance whether shelters accept people needing electricity for medical devices.  
- ☐ Confirm alternate care and refill options: call your pharmacy and regular clinic now to ask about emergency refills and nearest alternate sites if your usual clinic is closed.  
- ☐ Prepare a “go bag” and transportation plan if evacuation becomes necessary: include medications (7-day supply if possible), copies of prescriptions, batteries, chargers, and contact numbers for doctors and caregivers.

## How to check local conditions and help
- Check county or city emergency management pages and your utility website for the latest local flood warnings, outage maps and lists of shelters or medical respite centers.  
- Monitor the National Weather Service and local emergency alert systems for flash-flood and evacuation notices.  
- If you or a household member depends on electricity for medical care, call your county emergency management office or utility today to confirm what registries or priority services exist in your area.
