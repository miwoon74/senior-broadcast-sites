## What local reporting found (what is happening)

The Sarasota Herald‑Tribune reported on November 8, 2026, that multiple in‑home care agencies in Sarasota County are struggling with staffing and recruitment, producing persistent vacancies. Those shortages are forcing some seniors to delay or forego services that support daily living—bathing, medication reminders and mobility assistance—and push families toward unpaid caregiving.

The reporting describes agency closures and longer waits for services. The story frames the shortfall as a direct threat to seniors’ independence and safety because when paid home‑health support is unavailable, families must absorb more tasks and costs.

## Why this matters for seniors and families

Home‑health workers enable many older adults to remain at home safely. When workers are unavailable:

- Families may face higher unpaid caregiving time and financial strain—AARP’s 2026 figure of $11,500 per year for family caregivers illustrates the average cost burden.  
- Caregiver burnout and stress increase when supports are lacking.  
- Delays in in‑home help can raise the risk of hospital readmission or prevent timely assistance after a fall or health decline.

The Sarasota reporting highlights these practical consequences at the local level, while pointing readers to resources and policy options.

## Practical steps for families in Sarasota County (what you can do)

- Use Eldercare Locator online or call 1‑800‑677‑1116 to find vetted local home‑health agencies, caregiver support programs, and aging‑services contacts.  
- If you face immediate gaps in paid care, consider short‑term options such as respite services, community programs, or mutual aid networks until a regular worker is found.  
- Check eligibility for state Medicaid home‑and‑community‑based services or veteran benefits if applicable; contact your state Medicaid office or VA regional office for program details.  
- Talk with your primary care provider or care manager about fall‑prevention and medication management strategies that reduce urgent needs when in‑home help is limited.  
- Document care needs (daily tasks, mobility limits, medication schedule) to make it faster for new aides to start work and to support any applications for formal programs.

Checklist — immediate actions
- ☐ Call Eldercare Locator: 1‑800‑677‑1116 or visit eldercare.acl.gov  
- ☐ Create a one‑page list of daily care needs and medications for any new caregiver  
- ☐ Ask your doctor about referrals to home‑based care programs or community services  
- ☐ Contact your state Medicaid office or VA benefits office to check for eligibility and waiting lists  
- ☐ Identify at least one backup plan (family member, neighbor, community volunteer) for short gaps in paid care

## Policy approaches and international notes (why some places differ)

The broadcast notes that other countries, notably Japan, have taken a combined approach—boosting training pipelines, offering incentives for elder‑care careers, and deploying assistive technologies—to address workforce shortages. Local policy responses can include recruitment incentives, training and certification supports, and investment in technology to make home care more efficient. The Sarasota reporting suggests the county is seeing the local effects of a broader workforce shortfall in home health.
