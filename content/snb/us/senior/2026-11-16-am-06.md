Have you heard your college-age grandchild talk about attending or hosting a "Friendsgiving" this week? If you worried for a moment that peer gatherings are replacing traditional family dinners, the latest consumer research offers reassuring news. **A national 2026 survey revealed that 52% of young adults aged 18 to 29 plan to celebrate Friendsgiving this season**, yet these gatherings serve as a joyful prelude to family festivities rather than a substitute. 

Understanding how the younger generation approaches holiday hospitality can help grandparents feel closely involved, offer meaningful support, and pass down beloved culinary traditions without feeling left out.

## Friendsgiving by the Numbers: A Growing Holiday Tradition

While holiday potlucks among roommates and classmates have existed informally for years, Friendsgiving has officially matured into a core seasonal milestone for young Americans. Research conducted in autumn 2026 highlights a significant generational divide in how widespread these peer feasts have become.

According to a national holiday survey released in November 2026 by Chicory, 52% of Gen Z adults plan to host or attend a Friendsgiving, compared to 30% across the broader adult population. Additional tracking by CivicScience recorded general adult participation between 21% and 30% nationwide, while 38% of Gen Z respondents identified Friendsgiving as an essential holiday tradition.

Rather than indicating a drift away from family life, these figures reflect a generation eager to practice independent hospitality. For young people living away from home, college students in dorms, or young professionals sharing apartments, Friendsgiving offers an opportunity to test their cooking skills, bond with peers, and express gratitude in a relaxed setting before packing their bags to travel home for traditional family meals.

## How Young Adults Are Reinventing Holiday Gatherings

The way young people celebrate Friendsgiving looks quite different from a classic formal Thanksgiving dinner. Instead of carving a whole roasted turkey at dusk, young hosts are adapting the holiday to fit tighter budgets, smaller apartment kitchens, and busy academic or work calendars.

Data from the Napa Cellars 2026 Trend Report reveals several distinct formats favored by young hosts:

| Gathering Style / Feature | Share of Young Hosts (2026) | Primary Appeal |
|---|---|---|
| Late Morning "Brunch-giving" | 35.6% | Relaxed daytime timing, easy breakfast-lunch crossover items |
| Afternoon Grazing Platters | 31.6% | Casual mingling without requiring full dining table setups |
| Potluck Format Sharing | 64% | Distributes grocery costs and cooking duties across roommates |
| Elevated Macaroni and Cheese | 40.0% | Must-have upgraded comfort dish featuring premium cheeses |

Economics play a substantial role in these choices. Over 64% of young participants rely on potluck formats to share grocery expenses, ensuring no single student carries an unfair financial burden. Furthermore, timing often shifts toward the early hours: 35.6% favor daytime brunch celebrations, while 31.6% prefer afternoon grazing spreads. 

When it comes to the menu, traditional whole turkeys frequently take a back seat to comfort foods that are simpler to prepare in modest ovens. A notable 40% of survey respondents singled out elevated macaroni and cheese—often prepared with artisanal cheeses or special seasonings—as their top must-have dish.

## What This Trend Means for Grandparents

Hearing that your grandchild is organizing a holiday meal with friends can occasionally prompt a twinge of worry: Are they growing apart from home? Are family customs fading away? 

The data suggests exactly the opposite. Friendsgiving is an additional celebration of community, not an abandonment of family roots. In fact, young adults frequently look to their parents and grandparents for culinary inspiration when deciding what to contribute to a shared potluck table. 

Stepping forward with gentle encouragement, a favorite recipe, or practical support allows you to bridge the generational divide naturally. When a grandchild proudly serves your signature side dish to their roommates, your family heritage takes center stage in their growing independent lives.

## Action Steps to Bridge the Generational Divide

If your grandchild is gearing up for an apartment or campus potluck, here are practical, low-pressure ways to show your love and stay connected:

- ☐ **Ask about their menu theme:** Send a quick text asking what kind of gathering they are attending—whether an afternoon grazing spread or a brunch—and ask what dish they volunteered to bring.
- ☐ **Share a handwritten signature recipe:** Text a photo or mail a card with your secret sweet potato casserole, stuffing, or baked macaroni recipe, complete with clear oven temperatures and simple steps.
- ☐ **Send a small grocery stipend:** A modest digital grocery card of $20 to $30 sent through their preferred supermarket app can relieve student budget strain when purchasing premium cheeses or fresh produce.
- ☐ **Offer to bake portable sides:** If your grandchild lives nearby, offer to bake a batch of homemade dinner rolls or cookies they can easily take along in a reusable container.
- ☐ **Explore youth cultural trends:** Review educational resources on contemporary trends at ypulse.com to gain fresh conversation starters before holiday visits.
- ☐ **Access intergenerational toolkits:** If you are seeking broader ideas for staying close across distance, explore toolkits from Generations United at gu.org or call their family resource desk at 202-289-3979.

Connecting across generations does not require complicated gestures. A quick text or phone call by Wednesday can give your grandchild the confidence and recipes they need to shine at their first independent holiday table, while keeping your family traditions alive and well.
