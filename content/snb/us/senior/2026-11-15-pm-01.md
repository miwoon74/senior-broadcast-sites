Could your monthly retirement check see a noticeable bump in the coming legislative session? For millions of older Americans managing tight household budgets, the prospect of federal relief is taking center stage in Washington. **Under a newly highlighted legislative push, eligible beneficiaries could receive an additional $2,400 each year—equivalent to an extra $200 per month.**

As Senator Bernie Sanders prepares to take the gavel of the Senate Finance Subcommittee on Social Security, the Vermont independent has reaffirmed that expanding retirement benefits under the Social Security Expansion Act (S.770) will be his top legislative priority. The proposal seeks to address persistent household expenses while confronting the looming financial deadline facing the federal trust funds. Here is a comprehensive look at the specific figures, the funding mechanism behind the legislation, how the United States compares with international pension models, and the immediate steps you can take to safeguard your earned benefits.

## Examining the Numbers: Benefit Expansion vs. Solvency Deadlines

The primary motivation behind the legislative effort is the financial strain felt by fixed-income households across the country. According to Social Security Administration data, a substantial share of older Americans rely on Social Security for a large portion of their monthly income. For these retirees, even slight increases in grocery prices, utility bills, and out-of-pocket medical expenses create significant monthly deficits.

At the same time, federal actuaries continue to warn about the long-term solvency of the program. According to the 2026 Social Security Trustees Report, the combined trust funds face projected depletion late in 2032. If lawmakers take no corrective action before that deadline, statutory rules would trigger an across-the-board automatic benefit reduction of 22 percent for all beneficiaries. 

The Social Security Expansion Act attempts to tackle both issues at once: delivering an immediate payment increase while eliminating the projected shortfall.

To understand how the proposal plans to generate revenue, it is helpful to look at how payroll taxes are currently collected:

- **Current payroll tax cap (2026):** Wage earners stop paying Social Security taxes once their annual income exceeds $184,500. Any dollar earned above that limit is completely exempt from the 12.4 percent payroll tax.
- **Proposed tax floor:** The proposed legislation would apply the 12.4 percent payroll tax to all earned income and net investment earnings above $250,000.
- **The "donut hole" provision:** Earnings between $184,500 and $250,000 would remain untaxed under the current baseline structure. Because this change targets higher income brackets, the reform would leave taxes completely unchanged for 93 percent of U.S. households.

The revenue generated from this high-income tax floor is designed to wipe out the trust fund deficit beyond 2032 while simultaneously financing the $200 monthly boost.

## How the Social Security Expansion Act Changes the Rules

Beyond raising monthly benefit checks and lifting the earnings cap, the Social Security Expansion Act proposes a fundamental shift in how annual cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs) are calculated.

Under existing procedures, annual adjustments are tied to general consumer indexes that measure goods and services consumed by active workers. S.770 proposes officially switching the annual adjustment formula to the Consumer Price Index for the Elderly (CPI-E). 

The rationale behind adopting the CPI-E formula centers on spending patterns:
1. **Targeted market basket:** Older Americans spend a far higher percentage of their total monthly income on prescription drugs, healthcare premiums, doctor visits, and home energy utilities than younger working families.
2. **Reduced purchasing power lag:** Because general inflation metrics give greater weight to items like technology, apparel, or transportation, standard annual adjustments often fail to match the real-world inflation seniors experience.
3. **Compounding stability:** Adopting the CPI-E is intended to better reflect the inflation experience of older households and could affect benefit growth over time; check the latest at https://www.bls.gov/cpi/ for details.

While the bill faces significant partisan division and debate in Congress, Sanders taking leadership of the Senate Finance Subcommittee on Social Security places benefit expansion and financing reform at the center of the legislative agenda.

## International Perspective: How Other Nations Adjust Pensions

Debates over retirement system solvency and inflation protections are not unique to the United States. Other nations utilize distinct structural approaches to ensure that senior pensions keep pace with the cost of living.

In Canada, for example, the public Old Age Security (OAS) pension is administered under a model that adjusts payments frequently to shield recipients from price volatility:

- **Quarterly adjustments:** Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC) automatically recalculates OAS benefit payments four times each year based on real changes in the Consumer Price Index. This prevents seniors from waiting a full twelve months for relief during periods of rising prices.
- **Older senior adjustment:** Recognizing that healthcare and support costs accelerate with age, Canada provides an automatic 10 percent pension increase for seniors aged 75 and older.
- **Income clawbacks rather than flat tax caps:** Unlike the American system of capping taxable wages, Canada implements an OAS recovery tax. This mechanism claws back pension disbursements only from individual retirees whose annual income exceeds $90,997 CAD.

These international differences demonstrate that while the United States relies heavily on an annual indexing schedule and capped payroll contributions, alternative methods exist to maintain purchasing power and balance program financing.

## Practical Steps to Verify Your Record Today

While Congress debates long-term solvency solutions and potential benefit boosts, the most important step any senior or near-retiree can take today is to ensure their administrative records are accurate. Your future payment calculations depend entirely on your recorded lifetime earnings.

Use this checklist to confirm your Social Security account details:

- ☐ Log into your personal "my Social Security" account on the official website at ssa.gov.
- ☐ Download your latest Social Security Statement and review your historical yearly earnings table for any missing or inaccurate wage years.
- ☐ Confirm that your listed mailing address, direct deposit instructions, and contact details are fully up to date.
- ☐ If you are married or widowed, review your projected individual benefit against potential spousal or survivor options.
- ☐ For assistance correcting earnings discrepancies, call the Social Security Administration toll-free at 1-800-772-1213 (or TTY 1-800-325-0778 to speak with a field office representative).

Verifying your earnings history now ensures that whether benefit calculations remain under current law or adjust under future legislation, you receive every dollar you have rightfully earned.
