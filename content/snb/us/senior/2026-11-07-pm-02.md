The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and international reports show that the United States has recorded more than **5,000** confirmed measles cases in 2026 — the largest annual total in **35 years**, and the highest since **1991**. This rise increases risks for unvaccinated people of all ages and can be particularly dangerous for infants and older adults with weakened immune systems.

## Why this matters to families and older adults

Measles is a highly contagious viral illness. In the current surge, public health agencies note outbreaks are concentrated in communities with low childhood MMR (measles‑mumps‑rubella) vaccination coverage. That raises the chance that unvaccinated people in any age group will be exposed.

For seniors this matters in two ways:
- Grandparents who host young grandchildren may be exposed if children are unvaccinated or part of an outbreak community.
- Older adults with weakened immune systems are at higher risk of severe complications if infected.

If you are unsure about vaccine or immunity status, the CDC guidance in the broadcast recommends confirming records with your primary care provider or your state immunization registry. The CDC information line is 800‑CDC‑INFO (800‑232‑4636).

## What you can do now: practical steps for seniors and families

- Check vaccination records
  - Ask your primary care provider or check your state immunization registry about your MMR vaccine status or evidence of immunity.
  - If records are missing and you were born after 1957, CDC guidance mentioned in the broadcast indicates adults without proof of immunity should consider vaccination. Confirm with your clinician.

- Protect infants and vulnerable household members
  - Infants who are too young for the complete MMR series and older adults with weakened immune systems are at higher risk; avoid close contact with anyone showing measles symptoms or who is linked to an outbreak area.

- If exposure is suspected
  - Contact your healthcare provider immediately for advice; public health recommendations may include testing, immune globulin for certain high‑risk individuals, or quarantine measures depending on timing and immune status.

- Ask about local public health actions
  - Some jurisdictions run catch‑up campaigns or targeted vaccination drives in schools and communities during outbreaks. Your local health department can tell you whether such actions are happening near you.

## Checklist: quick actions to take this week

- ☐ Check your MMR vaccination records with your doctor or state registry.
- ☐ If unsure about immunity and born after 1957, discuss MMR vaccination with your clinician.
- ☐ Ask parents of visiting grandchildren about their vaccination status before close contact.
- ☐ If exposed or symptomatic, call your healthcare provider and avoid visiting high‑risk people (infants, immunocompromised, and elderly until cleared).
- ☐ Look up local health department advisories about measles activity and vaccination clinics.

## Policy and prevention context (what the reporting highlights)

The broadcast notes that U.S. measles outbreaks are occurring where childhood MMR vaccination rates are low. Public health practice after past surges has included school‑based catch‑up vaccination campaigns and focused outreach in communities with gaps in coverage. These are the standard tools used to curb outbreaks when cases rise.

For seniors, the immediate protective measures are individual vaccination verification and minimizing exposure risks around unvaccinated infants and immunocompromised people.
