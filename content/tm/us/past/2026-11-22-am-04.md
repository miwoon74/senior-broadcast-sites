39 years ago today, November 22, 1987, television screens across the Chicago metropolitan area abruptly cut away from scheduled programming. In their place appeared a bizarre, masked figure rocking back and forth before thousands of bewildered living rooms.

## The First Signal Intrusion at WGN-TV

The intrusion began on Sunday night during the sports segment of WGN-TV's The Nine O'Clock News. Anchor Dan Roan was in the middle of recapping the day's Chicago Bears football game when the studio feed abruptly cut to total black at 9:14 p.m. Moments later, an individual wearing a rubber Max Headroom mask and dark sunglasses appeared on home television screens. The figure swayed erratically in front of a semi-swiveling sheet of corrugated metal designed to mimic the computer-generated geometric background of the popular television character. A loud, garbled buzzing sound droned from the audio track, as the hijackers were unable to transmit clear speech. The intrusion lasted about thirty seconds in total, with nearly seventeen seconds of the pirate video visible to viewers. Engineers at WGN-TV acted quickly by changing the frequency of the studio transmitter link directed at the transmitter atop the John Hancock Center. When control returned to the studio, Roan remarked to viewers, 'Well, if you're wondering what's happened, so am I,' before joking that the station's computer had taken off and gone wild.

## A Second Attack During Doctor Who

Station managers and viewers assumed the strange disruption was an isolated technical glitch or a brief prank. However, roughly two hours later, the intruder struck again. At approximately 11:20 p.m., the broadcast of local Chicago PBS member station WTTW was interrupted while airing part one of the Doctor Who serial Horror of Fang Rock. Unlike the WGN studio, WTTW had no technical staff stationed at its broadcast tower at that late hour. Without personnel on hand to override the pirated link immediately, the perpetrators took full control of the airwaves for approximately ninety seconds. This second broadcast succeeded where the first had failed by transmitting audible, though heavily distorted, sound to thousands of homes across the metropolitan area.

## Ninety Seconds of Surreal Pirate Broadcasting

During the minute and a half on WTTW, the disguised figure delivered a disjointed monologue filled with strange cultural references and erratic laughter. The impersonator shouted about 'nerds,' called WGN sports anchor Chuck Swirsky a 'frickin' liberal,' and flashed a middle finger concealed inside an object. In another erratic moment, the speaker waved a can of Pepsi before the lens, mocking the 'Catch the wave' advertising slogan used in recent Coca-Cola commercials that had starred the real Max Headroom character. The broadcast continued as the intruder sang the phrase 'Your love is fading,' hummed the melody from the 1959 animated television series Clutch Cargo, and shouted, 'I still see the X!' referring to that show's final episode. The bizarre transmission concluded with the figure pulling down his trousers, exposing his bare buttocks to an off-screen woman who struck him repeatedly with a flyswatter. Shortly after that sequence, the hijackers cut their signal, and normal programming returned to the PBS broadcast.

## The Federal Investigation Goes Cold

Overpowering commercial and public broadcast transmitters was not just an elaborate prank; it was a serious federal offense. The Federal Communications Commission immediately launched a criminal investigation in cooperation with law enforcement agencies. Investigators sought to understand how the culprits generated sufficient microwave transmission power to override the stations' primary signals without being detected. While theories circulated regarding former broadcast employees, disgruntled technicians, or university computer hobbyists with access to advanced transmission equipment, hard evidence proved elusive. The FCC interviewed technicians and tracked potential transmission sites around Chicago, but no suspects were ever detained.

## An Enduring Legacy in Television History

Decades later, the Max Headroom broadcast intrusion remains an enduring piece of modern folklore and one of the rare television signal hijackings that was never solved. Decades of internet sleuthing, unofficial inquiries, and public fascination have failed to confirm the identities of either the masked figure or the technician behind the transmitter. The incident stands as a chilling and absurd reminder of television's analog era, when a homemade studio and a pirate signal could capture the screens of an entire major American city.
