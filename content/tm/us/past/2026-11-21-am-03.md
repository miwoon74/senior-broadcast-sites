41 years ago today, November 21, 1985, federal authorities arrested United States Navy civilian intelligence analyst Jonathan Pollard for espionage. The arrest brought an abrupt end to an unauthorized operation that saw thousands of highly classified pages transferred into foreign hands. Pollard had spent months copying and selling sensitive intelligence, ultimately triggering one of the most contentious espionage scandals between the United States and an allied nation.

## The Day Federal Authorities Closed In

On November 21, 1985, federal counterintelligence investigators apprehended Jonathan Jay Pollard. Suspicions had mounted around the analyst's unauthorized access to classified files that bore no relation to his normal duties. When investigators moved in, they discovered that Pollard had systematically funneled extensive American intelligence on Arab nations to Israeli contacts. The apprehension halted an active pipeline of secret material and exposed significant gaps in internal security protocols.

## A Background in Intelligence Access

Pollard was born in Galveston, Texas, in 1954, the youngest of three siblings. His father was an award-winning microbiologist who taught at the University of Notre Dame after the family moved to South Bend, Indiana, in 1961. Growing up with an intense interest in Zionism and Israeli affairs, Pollard later found employment as a civilian intelligence analyst within the United States Navy. That post granted him high-level clearances and wide-ranging access to classified archives across multiple military and civilian agencies.

## A Compromise of Vital State Secrets

Starting in 1984, Pollard began selling voluminous state secrets. Among the compromised materials was the National Security Agency's comprehensive ten-volume manual outlining the exact methods the United States uses to gather signal intelligence. Pollard also revealed the identities of thousands of individuals who had cooperated with American intelligence agencies. In defense of his actions, Pollard claimed the American intelligence community had endangered Israel's security by withholding critical files. However, prosecutors revealed that Pollard had also admitted shopping his clandestine services to other foreign nations.

## Plea Agreement and a Life Sentence

Following his arrest in 1985, Pollard entered into a plea agreement with the federal government. He formally pleaded guilty to espionage and providing top-secret classified information to a foreign government in violation of the Espionage Act. In 1987, despite hopes for a lesser term under the plea deal, a federal judge sentenced Pollard to life imprisonment. The sentence reflected the extraordinary volume and sensitivity of the compromised records, which intelligence officials warned had caused lasting harm to national defense.

## Bipartisan Pushback Against Early Clemency

Over the decades Pollard spent in prison, Israeli officials and various advocacy organizations repeatedly lobbied American administrations for a sentence reduction or presidential clemency. The Israeli government issued a formal apology to the United States in 1987, granted Pollard Israeli citizenship in 1995, and officially admitted paying him in 1998. However, numerous high-ranking American officials stood firmly against any clemency. Leaders including former defense secretaries Donald Rumsfeld and Dick Cheney, former CIA director George Tenet, and a bipartisan group of congressional representatives argued that Pollard's disclosures had severely undermined American sources and methods.

## Parole and Relocation to Jerusalem

Pollard served thirty years in federal custody before being granted parole on November 20, 2015. Under the conditions of his release, he remained subject to strict travel restrictions for five years. On November 20, 2020, his parole terms officially expired, lifting all remaining travel bans. Weeks later, on December 30, 2020, Pollard departed the United States, relocated to Israel, and settled in Jerusalem. More than four decades after his arrest, the Pollard case endures as a stark study of insider espionage and the enduring complexity of intelligence relations between allies.
