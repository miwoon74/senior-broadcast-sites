26 years ago today, November 17, 2000, Universal Pictures released the live-action holiday comedy How the Grinch Stole Christmas in movie theaters across the United States. Starring Jim Carrey in full prosthetic makeup and directed by Ron Howard, the film marked a historic moment in holiday cinema as the first live-action feature adaptation of a Dr. Seuss book.

## From Childhood Classic to Hollywood Blockbuster

Theodor Seuss Geisel, known to readers around the globe as Dr. Seuss, first published How the Grinch Stole Christmas! in 1957. By the time of Geisel's death in September 1991, his catalog of more than 60 books had sold upwards of 600 million copies and had been translated into more than 20 languages. Works like The Cat in the Hat, Green Eggs and Ham, and Horton Hears a Who! had formed the backbone of American bedtime reading for generations.

While television audiences had cherished Chuck Jones's 1966 animated special for over three decades, bringing the crooked peak of Mount Crumpit and the eccentric town of Whoville into a live-action theatrical format had long seemed almost impossible. Universal Pictures committed to an ambitious budget of 123 million dollars to turn the slender rhyming picture book into a sweeping visual spectacle.

## Ron Howard and Jim Carrey Take on Mount Crumpit

To steer the massive production, the studio enlisted director Ron Howard, working from a screenplay written by Jeffrey Price and Peter S. Seaman. For the central role of the green, misanthropic hermit who despises Christmas and plotted to steal it, there was one natural choice: Jim Carrey. Carrey was the undisputed king of physical comedy, having broken box-office records with hits like Ace Ventura: Pet Detective, The Mask, and Dumb and Dumber.

Narrated with dignity by Anthony Hopkins, the film expanded the Grinch's backstory, tracing his childhood traumas in Whoville and his unrequited feelings for classmate Martha May Whovier, played by Christine Baranski. Taylor Momsen stepped into the role of young Cindy Lou Who, whose kind-hearted curiosity challenges Mayor Augustus MayWho, played by Jeffrey Tambor, and sparks the Grinch's eventual transformation.

## A Grueling Transformation and Academy Recognition

Bringing the Grinch to life demanded extraordinary physical discipline. Carrey had to endure hours in the makeup chair each filming day, covered in thick latex appliances, yak hair, and full-coverage facial prosthetics. The actor's face was completely transformed, yet his trademark elastic expressions and manic energy still radiated through the layers of green latex.

That immense backstage labor did not go unnoticed by the industry. At the 73rd Academy Awards, How the Grinch Stole Christmas captured the Oscar for Best Makeup and Hairstyling. The film also secured nominations for Best Art Direction and Best Costume Design, reflecting the sheer scale and ingenuity required to construct the fantastical world of Whoville from scratch.

## The Box-Office Champion of 2000

Premiering in Los Angeles on November 8, 2000, before rolling out nationwide on November 17, the film immediately captured the imagination of American moviegoers. Although film critics offered mixed reviews regarding its darker backstory and expanded comedic interludes, audiences arrived in droves throughout the Thanksgiving and Christmas corridors.

The film ultimately accumulated 351 million dollars worldwide, finishing as the single highest-grossing film of the year 2000 at the domestic box office and the sixth-highest-grossing film globally. Furthermore, it stood as the fourth-highest-grossing holiday release in box-office history, trailing only the beloved Home Alone series.

## An Unshakable Holiday Legacy

In the decades since its November 2000 theatrical run, Ron Howard's adaptation has cemented its place in American popular culture. Jim Carrey's razor-sharp quips, exaggerated sneers, and chaotic physical set pieces became instant holiday memes and dialogue quoted by parents and children alike each winter.

Dr. Seuss's original 1957 story was built on the timeless realization that Christmas does not come from a store, but means a little bit more. Through Carrey's vivid performance and Howard's elaborate production, that timeless message reached an entirely new generation of viewers, ensuring that the grumpy green creature atop Mount Crumpit remains an inescapable part of the holiday season.
